# 2. His view on AGI

One of the strongest points was that **AGI is poorly defined**.

His position was essentially:

> Before asking "When will AGI arrive?", first define what AGI means.

Rather than predicting an AGI timeline, he is more interested in:

* How AI capabilities evolve.
* How existing AI can be deployed productively.
* How AI can contribute to areas such as:

  * climate change
  * drug discovery
  * education
  * productivity

He also pointed out that AI development has already progressed much faster than many people expected, making long-term predictions unreliable.

**Takeaway:** Don't spend too much energy predicting AGI. Understand and use the capabilities that exist now.

---

# 3. AI regulation is becoming an international problem

AI systems increasingly involve **multiple countries and organizations**.

For example:

**US company → AI model → German company → HR application → government/customer**

This creates questions such as:

* Which country's laws apply?
* Who is responsible when something goes wrong?
* How do obligations move through the AI supply chain?
* How can countries allow AI-related trade while maintaining safety?

He described AI as a stack:

**Compute → Data → Model → Application**

In the future, different layers of this stack could effectively become tradable resources.

This means countries will need some **harmonization of policies and standards**.

He mentioned the NIST AI Risk Management Framework as an example of a framework that can help create common ground.

---

# 4. India vs US vs EU approaches

The discussion contrasted different regulatory approaches.

### EU

The EU has moved toward a more formal, horizontal regulatory framework through the **AI Act**.

### US

The professor characterized the US approach as more **market-oriented**, with existing sector-specific laws playing an important role.

### India

He described India as still evolving its AI policy approach and suggested it appears more market-oriented than the EU approach.

An important concept he raised was the **"Brussels effect."**

Companies that want to operate in the large European market need to comply with EU rules. Those companies may then apply similar standards elsewhere because maintaining completely different systems can be inconvenient.

# 6. What could be transformative in the next few years?

He deliberately avoided making strong predictions because AI has developed much faster than expected.

But he identified two areas he finds particularly interesting.

### A. On-device AI

Today, many AI systems rely on cloud infrastructure.

He expects increasingly capable AI to run:

**directly on phones/devices → without constant cloud connectivity**

This could create many new applications.

This is especially interesting because smaller models could become capable of doing sophisticated tasks locally.

### B. AI + Energy

AI requires enormous amounts of computing power and therefore energy.

So there is a two-sided problem:

**AI → consumes energy**

but potentially:

**AI → helps optimize energy systems and decarbonization**

This creates significant research and engineering opportunities.

# 7. Who decides what "good AI" means?

An important discussion concerned **quality of service**.

Who decides what level of AI quality is acceptable?

Possible sources include:

* Society/community
* Individuals
* Companies
* Policymakers
* Laws/regulations

There isn't necessarily one universal answer.

The acceptable level depends heavily on the **application**.

For example:

An AI generating entertainment images may tolerate some errors.

An AI involved in:

* healthcare
* employment
* loans
* criminal justice

requires much stronger reliability and fairness considerations.

So:

**AI quality requirements are context-dependent.**

# 8. Can we trust companies and governments with our data?

The professor framed this as a **trade-off**.

For example, people use:

* WhatsApp
* Instagram
* Facebook
* Google

and receive services in exchange for allowing companies access to various forms of data.

The important point was:

> There is effectively a trade between data/privacy and services.

He contrasted this with India's digital public infrastructure model.

### US-style model discussed

Large private companies can accumulate large amounts of user data and build services around that data.

### India-style model discussed

Government-created digital infrastructure can provide foundational identity/payment infrastructure, while private companies compete to provide services on top.

The example discussed was:

**India Stack → UPI → PhonePe / Google Pay / etc.**

The underlying idea is the distinction between:

**Who provides the service?**

and

**Who controls/owns the underlying data?**

# 9. AI literacy in India

This was another particularly useful section.

The professor argued that AI literacy isn't just about teaching people what ChatGPT is.

There are several layers:

### 1. Basic digital access

People need access to:

* Internet
* Devices
* affordable connectivity

### 2. Affordable AI

AI services need to become inexpensive enough to reach large populations.

### 3. Local languages

AI education and AI applications should work in:

* Hindi
* Tamil
* Telugu
* Malayalam
* etc.

rather than assuming English proficiency.

### 4. Useful applications

AI literacy becomes much more meaningful when people see practical benefits.

Examples:

* agriculture
* education
* children's learning
* livelihoods

He mentioned **Khan Academy's AI tutor, Khanmigo**, as an example of AI potentially helping education scale.

The idea is:

**AI + teacher → teacher potentially supporting many more students**

rather than simply:

**AI → replaces teacher**

# 10. AI and warfare

He acknowledged that AI is already relevant to military applications.

Potential applications include:

* autonomous systems
* drones
* information warfare
* cyber warfare
* offensive capabilities

He suggested that the US-China relationship could eventually require something analogous to agreements around nuclear proliferation.

But he didn't suggest that AI development should simply stop.

Instead, the discussion was about **international governance of powerful technology**.

# 11. Representation and bias in AI training data

A very important technical/social issue discussed:

### What happens when people aren't represented in the training data?

Suppose an AI is primarily trained on digitally available information.

People who:

* don't use social media
* don't publish online
* speak less-digitized languages
* live in poorly digitized regions

may be underrepresented.

Therefore:

**Training data ≠ necessarily representative of society.**

This can create problems in AI outputs.

The professor argued that this is one reason countries such as India may want to develop **sovereign AI capabilities**, particularly because India has:

* huge linguistic diversity
* large populations
* unique datasets
* unique social contexts

# 12. Where are the career opportunities in AI?

This was probably one of the most practical sections.

The professor argued that people shouldn't focus only on:

> "Who will build the next LLM?"

There may actually be enormous opportunities in **operationalizing existing AI**.

Think of the AI opportunity as a pyramid:

### Top

**Create new AI models**

Fewer organizations and people operate here.

### Bottom

**Apply and operationalize AI**

Much larger opportunity.

Examples:

* AI for healthcare
* AI for railways
* AI for education
* AI for manufacturing
* AI for finance
* AI for enterprises
* AI for government

The fundamental question becomes:

> "How do I take AI and actually make it useful in a real organization?"

This is an extremely important distinction:

**AI research ≠ AI implementation**

There will be huge demand for people who understand a particular domain **and** understand how to apply AI to that domain.

# 13. Fairness isn't a single number

The discussion around fairness scores was interesting.

He emphasized that fairness isn't simply:

> "Calculate fairness score → if score is good → deploy AI."

Different fairness metrics can conflict.

He referenced the **fairness impossibility theorem**: you generally cannot satisfy every desirable fairness criterion simultaneously.

Therefore, organizations must determine:

* Which fairness criterion matters?
* In which context?
* What threshold is acceptable?
* What are the consequences of errors?

Fairness is also only one part of responsible AI.

Other dimensions include:

* reliability
* robustness
* efficiency
* safety
* human oversight

# 14. AI regulation: vertical vs horizontal

This was one of the most useful conceptual frameworks in the talk.

### Vertical regulation

Existing laws governing a particular industry are applied to AI.

Examples:

**AI + employment → employment laws**

**AI + lending → financial/credit laws**

**AI + healthcare → healthcare laws**

The important point:

> There isn't necessarily an "AI exception" to existing laws.

If an AI system makes a discriminatory lending decision, saying *"the AI made the mistake"* doesn't automatically eliminate the organization's legal responsibility.

### Horizontal regulation

Rules apply across AI applications regardless of the specific industry.

For example:

* high-risk AI
* medium-risk AI
* low-risk AI
* prohibited AI applications

The EU AI Act was discussed as an example of this type of approach.

---

# 15. Who should be responsible when AI causes harm?

His answer was essentially:

Don't start by asking:

> "Who should we punish?"

Instead ask:

1. What law applies?
2. Who deployed the AI?
3. What was the intended use?
4. What level of reliability was required?
5. What testing was performed?
6. What fairness criteria were appropriate?
7. Was there human oversight?
8. Who bears the cost when the system fails?

This leads to a more useful concept:

### Accountability must be designed into AI deployment.

Organizations should understand:

**Who is responsible → when something goes wrong.**

# 16. Human-in-the-loop is important

Especially for high-impact decisions, the question isn't simply:

**AI or human?**

There are several possibilities:

### AI as decision support

AI recommends → human decides.

### Human override

AI recommends → human can override when appropriate.

### Automated decision

AI makes the decision without human involvement.

The appropriate approach depends on:

* reliability
* consequences of errors
* application
* legal requirements
* fairness
* risk

---

# 17. Current state vs aspirational state

The professor made an interesting point here.

We shouldn't always optimize AI systems purely around:

> "What is possible today?"

Because AI is a **dynamic system**.

What happens today affects what happens tomorrow.

For example:

**AI deployed today → changes user behavior/data → affects future AI systems**

Therefore, sometimes an **aspirational target** is more appropriate than simply reproducing today's conditions.

But again, it depends on the prompt/context.
